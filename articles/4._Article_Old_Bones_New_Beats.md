OLD BONES, NEW BEATS

Engineering comfort into buildings that remember everything

Singapore’s skyline shimmers with glass and steel. But between the towers stand quiet survivors: buildings that hold the city’s memory in their masonry, their timber joists, their narrow five-foot ways. Most people walk past without a second thought. For an engineer, they demand a very different kind of thinking.

Bringing these properties back to life requires creativity, care and respect. It is preservation and progress at once: modern systems carefully woven into century-old frameworks without erasing their stories.

Protecting the City’s Architectural DNA

Singapore’s approach to building conservation is grounded in legislation. The Preservation of Monuments Act, enacted in December 1970, established the legal framework to protect buildings and sites of national significance, leading to the formation of the Preservation of Monuments Board. This early legislation ensured that landmarks of historical, architectural and cultural importance could be formally gazetted and safeguarded.

The broader urban conservation regime emerged in 1989, when the Urban Redevelopment Authority formalised its conservation programme and introduced statutory planning controls for entire precincts. Historic areas such as Chinatown and Little India were among the first to be gazetted, marking Singapore’s shift toward area-based conservation that protects streetscapes as well as individual buildings. Since then, more than 7,000 buildings have been conserved.

Preservation and the Modern City

As cities grow denser and more vertical, the pressure to rebuild and replace is constant. Yet the preservation of historic buildings plays a role in maintaining cultural depth, human scale and a sense of identity. Conservation allows the past and present to coexist, creating places that feel rooted and distinctive rather than anonymous and interchangeable.

In Singapore, this role carries particular weight. A multicultural, multi-ethnic society shaped by waves of migration, the city’s built heritage reflects the lived histories of its communities. Preserving districts such as Chinatown, Little India and Kampong Glam maintains a shared identity that binds communities together, ensuring that rapid urban change does not erase the physical and cultural traces of the past.

Where Mint Meets Heritage: 21 Keppel Road and 18 Cross Street

Mint Building Services delivered the complete mechanical and electrical scope across both 21 Keppel Road and 18 Cross Street, two projects located within URA-designated conservation contexts. In each case, delivery was shaped by strict constraints on retained façades, roof forms and structural fabric, requiring services strategies to be developed around the existing buildings rather than imposed upon them.

21 Keppel Road was originally constructed in the early twentieth century as the Customs Operations Command, an administrative and operational hub linked to Singapore’s port and maritime trade. Designed in a restrained colonial style, the building reflects a period when civic architecture was both functional and symbolic.

The conserved shophouses at 18 Cross Street date back to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, forming part of Singapore’s historic commercial core. Typical of shophouse developments from this period, they were built to support trade, commerce and community life, with narrow frontages, sheltered five-foot ways and robust masonry construction. Today, these retained buildings sit within a larger mixed-use development. The streetscape of Cross Street is preserved, and the area’s long tradition continues as a place of business and exchange.

Delivering Safely in Conserved Structures

Working within historic buildings brings challenges that extend beyond design and delivery. Structures built decades ago, and in some cases more than a century ago, were never intended to accommodate modern loading, services density or contemporary construction methodologies. Hidden structural weaknesses, material degradation and undocumented alterations are common, making early surveys, intrusive investigations and careful sequencing essential.

There is also an elevated risk of hazardous materials, particularly asbestos, which was widely used in building construction well into the late twentieth century. In conservation projects, asbestos may be concealed within ceiling voids, service risers, floor finishes or fireproofing systems, requiring specialist surveys, controlled removal and strict compliance with safety regulations.

Delivering works in these environments depends on proactive risk identification, close coordination with specialists and a disciplined approach to safety that respects both the building’s fabric and the people working within it.

Navigating Complexity

Delivering modern building services within historic structures sits at the intersection of engineering, regulation and stewardship. As conservation continues to shape Singapore’s urban fabric, these projects demand teams with experience navigating technical complexity, regulatory nuance and the realities of working within aged structures.

The old bones hold stories. The new beats – the ACMV systems, the power backbones, the fire protection networks threaded carefully through heritage walls – keep those stories liveable. That is the work.
