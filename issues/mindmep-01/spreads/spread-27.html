Preservation and the modern city

As cities grow denser and more vertical, the pressure to rebuild and replace is constant. Yet the preservation of historic buildings plays a role in maintaining cultural depth, human scale and a sense of identity. Conservation allows the past and present to coexist, creating places that feel rooted and distinctive rather than anonymous and interchangeable.

In Singapore, this role carries particular weight. A multicultural, multi-ethnic society shaped by waves of migration, the city’s built heritage reflects the lived histories of its communities. Preserving districts such as Chinatown, Little India and Kampong Glam maintains a shared identity that binds communities together, ensuring that rapid urban change does not erase the physical and cultural traces of the past.

WHERE MINT MEETS HERITAGE
21 Keppel Road and 18 Cross Street
Mint Building Services delivered the complete mechanical and electrical scope across both 21 Keppel Road and 18 Cross Street, two projects located within URA-designated conservation contexts. In each case, delivery was shaped by strict constraints on retained façades, roof forms and structural fabric, requiring services strategies to be developed around the existing buildings rather than imposed upon them.

21 Keppel Road was originally constructed in the early twentieth century as the Customs Operations Command, an administrative and operational hub linked to Singapore’s port and maritime trade. Designed in a restrained colonial style, the building reflects a period when civic architecture was both functional and symbolic.

The conserved shophouses at 18 Cross Street date back to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, forming part of Singapore’s historic commercial core. Typical of shophouse developments from this period, they were built to support trade, commerce and community life, with narrow frontages, sheltered five-foot ways and robust masonry construction.
